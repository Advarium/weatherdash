GOES-West IR▾
NOAA GOES-West (GOES-18) ABI Band 13 (10.3 µm) Clean Infrared via NASA GIBS. Covers eastern Pacific, Alaska, and western CONUS. 10-min scan cadence, ~40-min GIBS lag. The dashboard redraws each tile by temperature so only cloud shows: brightness is cloud-top temperature, not water vapour.
Cloud-top brightness temperature — clouds only
−90−60−40−20010 °C
Clear ground and warm low cloud are transparent. The colder the cloud top, the brighter and more opaque it is drawn: pale grey is mid-level cloud (−20 to −40 °C), and near-white is deep convection, cirrus shields and hurricane eyewalls (below −60 °C).
Enhanced colours (Infrared style: Enhanced)

Air Mass RGB▾
GOES-West, GOES-East and Himawari Air Mass RGB via NASA GIBS: a composite of two water-vapour channels, an ozone channel and infrared. It shows the moisture and temperature of the middle and upper atmosphere rather than the clouds themselves. Counts as a colour layer.
Red / orange: dry, descending upper air (jet streams, stratospheric intrusions)
Green / olive: warm, moist tropical air mass
Blue / purple: cold polar air mass
White: thick, cold high cloud
Dark blue: mid-level cloud
A sharp red edge next to cloud often marks a jet streak or a developing storm.
GeoColor▾
GOES-West and GOES-East GeoColor via NASA GIBS (not produced for Himawari). By day, near true colour: blue ocean, green and tan land, white cloud, grey smoke and dust. At night, infrared cloud over a static city-lights background: low cloud blue-grey, high cloud white. Counts as a colour layer.
GRACE Groundwater▾
NASA GRACE-FO Liquid Water Equivalent Thickness — total terrestrial water storage anomaly (groundwater + soil + surface water + snow/ice) relative to the 2004–2009 baseline. Monthly product; ~3-month lag. Via NASA GIBS.
Anomaly (cm LWE)
−30−15−3+3+15+30 cm
Brown = deficit (less water than the 2004–2009 baseline; drought signal) · transparent = within ±3 cm of normal · blue = surplus (recharge / flooding). Mascons over the ocean carry values too.

Sea Surface Temp▾
NASA/JPL MUR (Multi-scale Ultra-high Resolution) L4 sea surface temperature — a 1 km daily gap-free analysis blending infrared and microwave satellite retrievals with in-situ observations. Land and sea ice are masked out (transparent). Via NASA GIBS.
Temperature (°C)
010182426 TC30 °C
Dark navy is cold water; lighter teal is warmer. The light rule at 26 °C marks the rough threshold of sea surface warm enough to fuel tropical cyclones. Land and sea ice are transparent.
Sea Ice Concentration▾
NASA/JPL MUR sea ice concentration, the companion mask to the MUR SST analysis. Shows the fraction of each cell covered by ice. Best viewed at polar latitudes. Via NASA GIBS.
Ice cover (%)
0155085100 %
Transparent below 15 % (the conventional ice-edge threshold). Pale blue-grey is loose pack; white is 100 % consolidated ice.
Total Column Ozone▾
NASA OMPS aboard Suomi NPP — total column ozone, the full amount of ozone in a vertical column of atmosphere, measured in Dobson Units. Built from roughly 14 orbits per day, which together cover the whole sunlit globe. The composite is assembled as orbits downlink, so this layer is pinned two UTC days back, where coverage is complete.
Ozone (Dobson Units)
100220 hole300400500 DU
A single violet ramp. The deepest, most opaque violet is depleted ozone: below the 220 DU rule is the conventional "ozone hole" threshold, seen over Antarctica in austral spring. Typical values (about 300 DU) are faint, and ozone-rich air fades toward transparent.
Sulfur Dioxide (SO₂)▾
NASA OMI aboard Aura — sulfur dioxide in the lower troposphere, in Dobson Units. Picks out volcanic degassing and eruption plumes as well as industrial emissions. Daily polar-orbiter product pinned to the previous UTC day; swath gaps are normal.
SO₂ (Dobson Units)
210172532 DU
Brighter means more SO₂: dark navy = background levels · mid blue = elevated · pale blue to white = heavy loading, typically a volcanic plume. Pairs well with the Volcanism layer for spotting which volcano is degassing.
Faint dashes and stripes scattered over open ocean and land are retrieval noise, not plumes — they sit at 2–3 DU, the very bottom of the scale, where this product has poor signal-to-noise. OMI has also carried a known row anomaly since 2007 (part of the detector's field of view is obstructed), which shows up as banding along the orbit tracks. A genuine plume is bright and spatially coherent, reaching 20 DU and above.
Surface Wind▾
Observed wind from ~5,600 airport weather stations (METAR) and ~900 ocean buoys (NDBC) worldwide, via Esri Living Atlas; reports older than 3 hours are hidden. One barb per area of the map: the station nearest its centre. Zoom in to see more stations, or search a station code (e.g. KLNK). Click a barb for temperature, dew point, pressure, visibility and sky; buoys add waves and water temperature.
Reading a barb
The staff points into the wind (the direction it is blowing from). Half barb = 5 kt · full barb = 10 kt · solid pennant = 50 kt · open circle = calm, or variable with no set direction.
Barbs are drawn in a single off-white with a dark outline, the usual meteorological style: speed is read from the barbs themselves, and the popup gives the exact value.
